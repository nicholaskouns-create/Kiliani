## The dating question

This is genuinely unsettled, and the range is not manuscript confusion — it is
competing historical interpretation of the same text.

| Position | Date | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Levison** (1910) | c. 840 | The text calls Pippin *first king of the East Franks*. That kingdom dates from Louis the German (833), which gives the *terminus post quem*. |
| **Bigelmair** | c. 752 | Composed around Burchard's elevation and translation of the relics. |
| ***Geschichtsquellen*** repertory (current) | 750–850 | With composition c. 788 remaining a serious proposal. |

The *terminus post quem* that nobody disputes is **752**, the translation, because
the text narrates it.

The archive's earlier plates asserted "ca. 752" as settled and printed it in the
title. That was one hypothesis presented as a fact, and the September 2026
revision removed it. See [recensions/](../../recensions/).

**Note the dependency:** Levison's argument rests entirely on the full royal
title, and this archive's Latin abridges it to a bare *Pippini regis*. The
abridgement removed the evidence for the dating. Until the full wording is
restored from Levison, this archive cannot weigh the two positions at all. See
[apparatus.md](apparatus.md) n. 8.

## What the text is, and is not

It is the earliest continuous narrative: Irish origin, companions, the Würzburg
mission, Roman authorisation, the ducal conflict, the martyrdom, and Burchard's
translation. It served as the source for the tenth-century
[*Passio maior*](../passio-maior/).

It is not an eyewitness account and not a seventh-century document. It was
probably written by a cleric of the Main region, at least decades and probably a
century after the events it narrates.

## Historical register of its contents

Following the four-tier grading in the [research report](../../scholarship/research-report.md):

**Robust.** Burchard was the first organised bishop of Würzburg. A Carolingian
cult of Kilian and two companions existed. The *minor* precedes the *maior*.

**Probable.** Memory of an Irish or Irish-associated missionary; activity near
Würzburg; two principal companions; violent death connected to conflict with a
ruling household.

**Possible but unverified.** That the dispute specifically concerned Gozbert's
marriage to his brother's widow; that Geilana personally ordered the killing;
that the bodies were concealed in a stable; that Burchard recovered the original
grave.

**Predominantly literary, or dependent on the Conon chronology.** Eleven
companions; the journey of 686; personal authorisation by Pope Conon; death
precisely on 8 July 689.

Two further critical observations that bear on reading the text:

- **Gozbert's people were probably already Christian.** The ducal family —
  the Hedenen — was Christianised in the seventh century. The narrative needed
  Kilian to be an apostle who converted a *gens*, so an existing Christian
  society was rhetorically returned to paganism. *Cum omni populo suo* is
  literary totality, not a census.
- **Geilana is a type as well as a person.** The hostile royal woman opposing the
  prophet is a hagiographic pattern (Jezebel, Herodias). Thornborough's reading of
  the *Passiones* as "textual arguments" applies directly. The marriage dispute
  may still preserve a real conflict: a rigorist cleric challenging a politically
  important affinity marriage that the local elite regarded as legitimate.

## Kilian was not the first bishop of Würzburg

Burchard was, consecrated before October 741, dead in 753. The Kilian legend
supplied the diocese with an older sacred prehistory, so that apostolic mission
and martyr blood preceded the canonical foundation. That is why these texts
mattered politically: the body became an institutional charter in relic form, and
the written passion explained why Würzburg possessed both apostolic antiquity and
episcopal legitimacy.
