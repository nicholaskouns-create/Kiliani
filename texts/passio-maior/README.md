## Date and authorship

The current *Geschichtsquellen des deutschen Mittelalters* repertory dates the
*maior* to approximately **950–970** and associates it, possibly, with **Stephen
of Novara**, an Italian scholar teaching at the Würzburg cathedral school from
952.

The attribution is **probable, not demonstrated**. There is no authorial
colophon. It rests on the Petersohn–Hoffmann line of analysis — anachronistic
titles, political terminology, literary dependence, codicological transmission.

Some twentieth-century scholarship placed the work in the later ninth century
instead. The disagreement is interpretive, not codicological.

## What the maior adds to the minor

Noble parentage. Education. Interior deliberation. Set speeches. Court drama.
Punishment of the antagonists. The discovery of the bodies. A much fuller account
of the growth of the cult.

Two additions matter structurally:

- **The Atalongus miracle.** A priest teaching boys hears the children cry out
  that Kilian is working miracles and must be raised from where he lies
  unworthily. Atalongus ignores the vision, is struck blind, is led to the tomb
  by a rustic, prays, and recovers his sight. The episode supplies the *inventio*
  — the mechanism by which a concealed grave becomes a public shrine.
- **The Boniface transition.** The fame of the martyrs reaches Boniface, who then
  investigates how the town holding their bodies might be raised to episcopal
  dignity. This welds the martyr cult directly to the founding of the bishopric.

The current repertory describes the work as characterised by the "addition of
individual biographical details, mostly probably invented" and by a "dramatic
exposition of the passion".

## Why it was written

The *minor* is a compressed institutional legend. The *maior* turns it into a
fully developed saint's life for a tenth-century audience: it promotes the saint
and reasserts the bishopric's standing in the Ottonian Reich.

Three timescales are joined in it — the remembered Merovingian missionary, the
Carolingian establishment of the see, and the Ottonian literary culture of the
cathedral school.

## Transmission

The *maior* circulated **far more widely than the *minor***: roughly **forty**
manuscripts or references on that scale, against a handful for the *minor*. The
expanded version is the one the Middle Ages actually read.
