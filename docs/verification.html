What was read, and from what

The PDF's text layer is OCR of 1896 antiqua and is not usable for collation. It confuses V/F, Sg/8g, M²/ilf", drops semicolons between readings, and breaks words across line-ends. A first pass over that layer attributed 2 readings to M² where the plate shows dozens.

Every entry in this dataset was therefore transcribed by eye from the page rendered at ~430 dpi. The verbatim string of each apparatus entry is kept in the verbatim field as the primary datum, so any record can be checked against the plate without re-reading the whole apparatus. Everything else — the split into readings, witness attribution, and type — is derived from that string by build_model.py and can be regenerated.

Emmerich's apparatus is mixed: he prints a normalised text and cites manuscript readings beside it, so a witness is named when it differs from what he set, and is silent when it agrees. Under that convention the citation count is an inverse measure of how closely the printed text follows a witness.

Sg is cited 24 times — the fewest of the seven, by a wide margin. Emmerich's base text is St Gallen 566, and the count says so without his having to.

W is cited 111 times — the most. Emmerich notes of that manuscript: “Aliquibus locis omittuntur, quae pertinent ad ornatum orationis.” The Würzburg copy abridges rhetorical ornament, and the apparatus registers every omission. The editor's prose note and the citation count agree, which is a small check that the extraction is sound.

Limit

This is a signal, not a stemma, and it must not be published as one. Three limits:

  1. The apparatus is mixed, so agreements with the base text are invisible. Sg's affinities are depressed by exactly the thing that makes Sg the base.
  2. Lachmannian stemmatics is built on shared errors, not shared readings. Emmerich does not mark which readings are errors, and nothing here infers it.
  3. Orthographic agreement (uuirziburc / wirzipurch / wirzeburg) is scribal habit and regional spelling, not descent. The parser tags orthographic where it can detect it, but the tagging is heuristic and should be reviewed before any of it carries weight.

A real stemma needs autopsy of the seven manuscripts, or at minimum IIIF images, with errors identified as errors. This dataset is the substrate for that work, not a substitute for it.

Worked example — p. 4, lemma 8

The adopted text reads:

propria contempni patriamque et parentes secundum Domini evangelium relinquere et nudos sequi Christum.

Sgs. IX propria contempni passive infinitive; the adopted reading
Ws. XI propria contempnere active
M²s. XII propria contemnere active, classical orthography
Ps. XIII–XIV propriam contempnere accusative singular; the object changes
Ads. XII secundum evangelium domini nudosque Christum sequi clause transposed

Contemnere and sequi hold across every witness. Propria does not. In the sentence that tells the companions to give up what is their own, what is their own is the word the transmission could not hold steady — and in P it stops being their possessions and becomes, by assimilation to the following patriam, their country.

Still open

  • IIIF. Every iiif field is null. St Gallen (e-codices), the ÖNB, and the BSB all serve IIIF; Admont partly; Würzburg and Pommersfelden would need enquiry. Filling those turns each citation into a link to the folio.
  • Line numbering — desideratum (d) — needs the printed text keyed line-by-line, not just by lemma.
  • Emendations vs manuscript readings — desideratum (c) is now possible rather than done: the data distinguishes what Emmerich printed from what each manuscript has, but where he emends silently against all witnesses, only autopsy will show it.
  • The orthographic tags are heuristic and want a philologist's pass.
