Iron and Gold
Executive summary
The two lineage plates do support a historically meaningful synthesis. On the maternal side, the strongest documentary core is the Augsburg Kilian craft dynasty: a goldsmith family that became one of the best-known engraving and printmaking houses of early modern Augsburg. On the paternal side, the strongest documentary core is the Nassau-Siegen Cuntze/Kuntze family that entered Virginia with the 1714 Germanna colony recruited for Governor Alexander Spotswood’s iron enterprise. In that narrow but important sense, “gold” and “iron” are not only metaphors; they correspond to real regional craft worlds grounded in Augsburg goldsmithing and engraving, and in Siegerland ironworking and toolmaking. [1]
The public evidence does not validate every step on the plates equally. St. Kilian is a real seventh-century missionary saint associated with Würzburg, but no public source consulted here establishes a genealogical bridge from him to the later Augsburg Kilian family. “St. Clegwallabog Killian” was not corroborated in the authoritative saint and Augsburg-family references reviewed here. The plate’s “Hans Killian (1516–1583)” also appears unstable: the strongest official match in Bavarian reference works is Hans Kilian the Neuburg/Passau printer with life dates around 1515?–1595, not a clearly identified Augsburg progenitor of the engravers. On the Kouns side, the line becomes far firmer at Joseph Jost Cuntze/Kuntze (1674–1731), whose emigration permission, 1724 importation proof, and 1730/31 will are all anchored in named record series. Earlier pre-1714 Cuntze generations remain plausible, but in this pass I could not independently verify all of them in digitized parish or guild records. [2]
The real convergence of the two lines belongs to the Appalachian and Ohio River world rather than to medieval Europe. Derivative but internally consistent evidence places the later Killion branch in Tennessee and Kentucky, while the Kouns branch moved through Virginia, Greenbrier, Ohio, and northeastern Kentucky. By the twentieth century, the plates’ own logic becomes historically concrete: Anna Rose Killion Kouns and Donald Ray Kouns join the two strands in Kentucky, and the two charts terminate in the same living endpoint, named maternally as Nicholas “Nick” Kouns and paternally as Nicholas Shane Kouns. [3]
Scope, method, and extracted names
This review prioritizes primary or official records where they were publicly surfaced, then museum/library authority records, then carefully labeled derivative genealogical syntheses when no stronger public record appeared. The certainty labels used below are straightforward: documented means anchored in a primary record or an authoritative museum/library/biographical reference; likely means consistent derivative reconstruction without the underlying record image inspected in this pass; speculative means uncorroborated or in tension with stronger evidence.

The Kilian line
The Kilian plate begins with a sacred origin, but the documentary record separates symbolic ancestry from provable blood descent. St. Kilian is a real missionary saint whose cult became central to Würzburg and East Franconia; the early vitae are themselves late and partly legendary, and the saint’s historical dating is not exact in the modern documentary sense. What the sources support is a devotional and onomastic root, not an unbroken genealogical chain from the seventh century to the later Augsburg Kilians. The publicly documented family story begins much later, in sixteenth-century Augsburg. [4]
That later story is unusually strong because Augsburg’s civic, artistic, and print culture is well catalogued. Augsburg’s copper engraving tradition grew out of techniques long associated with metalworking and goldsmith engraving, and the city lexicon places the first Augsburg copper engravings around 1570. The Kilian family enters that world through Bartholomäus Kilian the Elder, described in Augsburg reference literature as a goldsmith from Silesia, born 1548, settled at Augsburg, and father of the first major engravers in the line, Lucas and Wolfgang. This matters for your “iron and gold” framing: the maternal line’s most secure early identity is not abstract nobility but technically skilled luxury craft. [5]
The family’s rise from precious-metal craft into print culture is exceptionally well documented. Lucas Kilian is recorded by the British Museum as painter, draughtsman, and engraver, born 1579 and dead 1637, trained within the Custos publishing milieu and seasoned by travel in Italy. Wolfgang Kilian, born 1581 and dead 1662, likewise trained under his stepfather Dominicus Custos and traveled in Italy; Augsburg sources credit him with the great 1626 map of Augsburg and major architectural and portrait work. The line is therefore not merely “artistic” in a vague sense; it belongs to the very specific early modern transition by which goldsmith-derived line work became commercial engraving, cartography, portraiture, architecture prints, and confessional image production. [6]
The confessional setting also matters. The Council of Trent defined Catholic reform and helped consolidate the visual regime of the Counter-Reformation, while post-Reformation Europe simultaneously generated intense Protestant print culture. Lucas Kilian’s surviving corpus shows that Augsburg workshops could work across that divide: the British Museum holds his anatomical flap broadsheets and a 1626 Luther broadside, while the Met preserves his engraving after the great St. Michael over Augsburg’s armoury, a work explicitly read there as tied to Counter-Reformation southern Germany. That is the “sacred and profane” structure in its most exact historical form: saint imagery, doctrine, portraiture, anatomy, city views, and print commerce all passing through one technically sophisticated urban workshop tradition. [7]
The later Kilian engravers sustain that documentary density. Philipp Kilian (1628–1693) is listed by the British Museum as an engraver, son of Wolfgang, who traveled to Italy in 1648. Augsburg’s city lexicon and the Deutsche Biographie entry for Philipp Andreas Kilian (1714–1759) show how the family kept renewing itself through travel, apprenticeship, portraiture, and publishing. Georg Christoph Kilian (1709–1781) is especially important because he was not only an engraver but also a family chronicler and collector; Augsburg’s city lexicon credits him with a set of 142 artist portraits and explicitly cites his family chronicle manuscript, Kunst und Ehrengedächtnis des Kilian’schen Geschlechts, in the Staats- und Stadtbibliothek Augsburg. In other words, the family itself became an archive. [8]
The American Killion stretch of the plate is much less securely documented in open public sources than the Augsburg engravers, but the general Appalachian direction of the chart is plausible. A derivative FamilySearch profile places William Killion (1768–1850) first in North Carolina and later in Warren and Claiborne counties, Tennessee, which fits the plate’s claim of transatlantic continuation into the southern uplands. A record-based family reconstruction of the Laurel/Claiborne Killion branch identifies John J. Killion (1813–1890) as William’s son and places his move from Claiborne County to Laurel County, Kentucky after 1849. The same reconstruction preserves Arthur L. Killion (1848– ) as a son of John and Matilda Lanham, but I did not independently confirm the plate’s full 1848–1920 life span for Arthur in public primary records during this pass. [9]
The modern endpoint is stronger again. Anna Rose Killion Kouns (1931–2015) is documented in funeral-home and obituary records as born in Lancaster, Kentucky, daughter of Alex and Elizabeth Hill Killion. FamilySearch likewise places her as daughter of Alex J. Killion. That makes the twentieth-century convergence of the two plates concrete even where some nineteenth-century Killion links remain to be proven in county-level records. [10]
The Kouns line
The Kouns plate is strongest where it is most specific: Niederndorf/Oberfischbach, guild work, 1713–1714 migration, and Virginia records. Historic Germanna traces the Cuntze/Kuntze/Coons family in that district back to around 1500, describes movement between Niederndorf and Oberfischbach, and places the line in a region where ironworking was economically defining. The same source states that Niederndorf had an ironworks dating to at least 1417, and that Johannes Kuntze, father of the 1714 immigrant, and all of his sons belonged to the steelsmiths’ and toolmakers’ guild. That is the clearest documentary foundation for the plate’s “ironworking and craft” theme. [23]
From Joseph Jost Cuntze/Kuntze onward the evidence becomes genuinely anchorable. Historic Germanna gives him as born in 1674 at Niederndorf, son of Johannes and Anna (Schuster) Cuntze, married in 1704 to Anna Gertrud Reinschmidt, and granted permission to emigrate on 31 July 1713. The Virginia Historical Magazine transcription of the Spotsylvania court records then shows Joseph Guntz [Coons] proving in 1724 that he came in April 1714 with Katherina his wife, his son John, and daughter Kathrina. The same Virginia source explains that the 1714 Germans were invited expressly to develop Spotswood’s iron properties and introduced comparatively modern iron-manufacturing methods in Virginia; Historic Germanna further cites Joseph’s 1730 will, probated 10 February 1731 in Stafford County. This is the documentary hinge on which the paternal plate turns from European guild craft into colonial migration. [24]
After Joseph Jost, the line broadens westward but becomes less cleanly documented in open-access web sources. Derivative genealogical materials place Georg Heinrich Koontz, Balthasar Koontz / Balser Counts, Christian Kouns / Kunz / Coontz, and John Lamp Kouns in the Shenandoah–Greenbrier–Ohio–Kentucky arc that the plate depicts. That trajectory is historically plausible. Kentucky’s rivers, especially the Ohio, were major migration arteries, and the Ashland–Ironton–Huntington corridor later became a tri-state industrial region built on river transport, furnaces, and rail connections. But exact person-by-person proof for several eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Kouns links still requires county-level deed, probate, church, and census work. [25]
The twentieth-century Kentucky terminus is clearer again. FamilySearch preserves a derivative but record-linked profile for Edgar Kouns (1902–1967). Donald Ray Kouns (1932–2003) is likewise preserved in FamilySearch, and the obituary of Anna Rose Killion Kouns confirms him as her deceased husband. The paternal and maternal twentieth-century lines therefore meet in the documentary present of northeastern Kentucky even if some earlier nineteenth-century steps require tighter archival proof. [26]
The woven chronology of iron and gold
Read together, the two plates tell a disciplined story, but not the one they tell at every point about themselves. The Kilian side is strongest as a story of sacred memory, goldsmith technique, engraving, and family archival consciousness. The Kouns side is strongest as a story of iron district craft, migration permissions, colonial labor transfer, and frontier movement through river valleys. What joins them is not medieval continuity proven all the way back, but the repeated survival of technically trained households through confessional upheaval, Atlantic migration, Appalachian settlement, and industrial reorganization. [38]
The chronology above shows the strongest validated spine. The maternal plate’s deepest secure historical layer is the Augsburg craft family; the paternal plate’s deepest secure historical layer is the Niederndorf/Germanna ironworking migration. The sacred beginning and the living endpoint belong to the same narrative, but the evidentiary density is concentrated in the middle: sixteenth- to eighteenth-century craft, print, migration, and settlement. [39]
Certainty map and prioritized research checklist
Three propositions are already strong. First, the Kilian side genuinely emerges from the world of Augsburg goldsmithing and engraving. Second, the Kouns side genuinely emerges from the world of Siegerland ironworking and the 1714 Germanna migration. Third, the two lines really do converge in modern Kentucky through the Killion–Kouns marriage represented on the plates. The weakest propositions are the medieval-sacred bloodline jump on the Kilian side, the exact identity of “Hans Killian (1516–1583),” the uncorroborated “St. Clegwallabog Killian,” and several nineteenth-century American transitions in both lines. [44]
A rigorous next archival pass should proceed in this order:
Resolve the Kilian origin problem.
Start with Stadtarchiv Augsburg, Staats- und Stadtbibliothek Augsburg, and the manuscript cited in the Augsburg lexicon, Georg Christoph Kilian, Kunst und Ehrengedächtnis des Kilian’schen Geschlechts. The immediate goal is to test whether the plate’s Hans Killian (1516–1583) is a real Augsburg lineal progenitor or a conflation with the better-documented Hans Kilian printer of Neuburg/Passau. Search terms should include Kilian, Killian, Hans Kilian, Bartholomäus Kilian, Goldschmied, Bürgerbuch, Meisterbuch, and Testament. [45]
Prove or disprove “St. Clegwallabog Killian.”
This is the most fragile name on either plate. Search it exactly, then search phonetic and orthographic neighbors in German and Latin saint lists and in private genealogical deposits. If no authoritative saint, parish, guild, or civic source emerges, the name should be downgraded from “ancestor” to “family tradition or unsupported insertion.” Comparative control sources are the Würzburg saint materials and the Augsburg Kilian family references already cited. [4]
Push the pre-1714 Cuntze line back into parish and tax records.
The target repositories are the church and regional archives for Oberfischbach / Niederndorf / Siegen-Wittgenstein, together with any surviving guild and tax material for the steelsmiths’ and toolmakers’ guild. The key record series to locate are the 1566 tax list attributed in derivative reconstructions to Henchin Cuntze, marriage and baptism entries for the 1667 Oberfischbach generation, and any guild admissions for Johannes/Jost and brothers. Search all variants: Cuntze, Kuntze, Kuntz, Koontz, Coons, Counts, Kouns, Kunz, Coontz. [46]
Tighten the Germanna-to-frontier chain with Virginia records.
At the Library of Virginia, inspect the actual image sets for Spotsylvania Will Book A, Stafford County Will Book 1729–48, land patents, Prince William/Fauquier records, and any Hebron Lutheran or associated church materials. The purpose is to move from “Joseph Jost is anchored” to “the next two generations are anchored with deeds, wills, and church records.” That is the step needed to make Georg Heinrich Koontz, Balthasar, and Christian more secure. [47]
Rebuild the American Killion strand from county records rather than derivative trees.
The crucial counties are Claiborne County, Tennessee, then Laurel, Garrard, Lincoln, and Boyle counties in Kentucky. Pull marriage books for John J. Killion, deed indexes for the 1849 move to Kentucky, probate and cemetery records for Arthur Killion, and the death certificate or death register entry for Alex J. Killion. Search variants Kilian, Killian, Killion, and, where handwriting is poor, phonetic neighbors. [48]
Resolve the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Kouns bottlenecks.
The most urgent unresolved paternal row is George W. Kouns (1863–1939), where same-name confusion is already visible in public material. Work outward from Edgar Kouns (1902–1967) and Donald Ray Kouns (1932–2003) using death certificates, obituaries, census households, probate, and cemetery records in Greenup, Boyd, Lawrence, and neighboring counties. Once George W. is cleanly identified, the Christian-to-George-to-Edgar sequence should become much firmer. [26]
The deepest historical truth in the plates is therefore narrower and stronger than the plates’ fullest claim. The sources sustain a family history rooted in Augsburg precious-metal and print craft on one side and Siegerland ironworking and Germanna migration on the other, with real continuity through Appalachia into Kentucky. They do not yet sustain every sacred or early-generational leap as a proven bloodline. That is not a collapse of the story. It is the story reduced to its hardest kernel: skilled households carrying technique, memory, and names across confessional Europe, the Atlantic, the frontier, and modern industrial Kentucky. [49]
